The problem

Pomodoro timers work, but they're joyless — a countdown you avoid looking at. This one inverts the incentive: the artwork is the interface. A public-domain painting starts hidden and is uncovered in lockstep with real elapsed time, so glancing at the canvas tells you how deep into the block you are — and every finished session ends with a small act of discovery, a masterpiece you may never have seen. Time itself is demoted to a small corner readout.

An always-connected reveal

Early prototypes revealed random squares — it read as dead pixels. The shipped design computes a Dijkstra arrival field: a flood spreads from a seed point (the center, a corner, or an edge, chosen per session) through a smooth random cost terrain. The revealed region grows through adjacency, so it is one connected organism by construction — it fingers and wanders like ink blooming through paper, but it can never leave disconnected islands.

Rendering punches soft overlapping circles out of a persistent veil canvas (incremental, one punch per cell), so edges read as organic blobs, never squares. Brass embers glow along the live frontier. The thresholds are normalized so the final cell lands exactly at 00:00 — the reveal can never outrun the timer.

  • Pure timer reducer. Elapsed time is injected as an action, so auto-advance, overshoot carry-over, and long-break cycles are unit-tested without clocks — and a throttled background tab can't drift the countdown.
  • The frame is the progress bar. A brass border draws itself clockwise around the painting, completing at 00:00 — then drains through the break while the finished piece stays up as the reward. A pulsing ember rides the tip.
  • Seeded determinism. Every pattern derives from a per-session seed (mulberry32), so a reveal never reshuffles between frames, and the same session always blooms the same way.

Decisions & trade-offs

Reveal tracks real time, not an animation
The displayed fraction eases toward true elapsed time each animation frame — smooth motion, but the canvas is honest: it is a progress bar in disguise, not a parallel effect.
Tainted canvas is acceptable
Some museum image hosts send no CORS headers. The loader tries a CORS-clean load first, then retries plain — and since the reveal never reads pixels back, a tainted canvas costs nothing. Failed images swap silently to a bundled piece; the previous painting keeps rendering while the next loads.
Whole-canvas effects were cut
Blur and pixelate reveals survived three design rounds, then lost: mid-session they read as a broken smudged image, not progress. Only the organic propagation patterns ship.
Breaks keep the finished piece
The reward for a completed block is looking at what you uncovered. New art arrives only when the next focus begins.

What I'd change at scale

  • Preload the next artwork during the current block so the phase flip is instant.
  • A small service worker caching recent artworks for fully offline sessions.
  • A shareable weekly "gallery" of everything you've revealed.
